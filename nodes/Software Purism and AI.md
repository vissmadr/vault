# Software Purism and AI

#empty

---

An argument that people have against AI is that "AI creates things instead of you".
This usually implies that without AI you would "create stuff" yourself.
When it comes to software development, this doesn't make much sense.

Some of it is true I guess.
But as a software engineer, I would argue that we never really "created ourselves" that much stuff before AI either.
We already had a non-AI level of automation that people with such strong opinions seem to forget about.
Now with AI the automation is just better than before.
Ridiculously better than before.

Purists would argue that writing code is an act of creation.
Sure, I can agree with some of that, but not to the fullest extent.

There were purists in the past, before AI existed, and back then they whined about low-level vs high-level software.
The mindset was always that the level of software is proportional to the size of your balls and brain, where lower means bigger.

So, with or without AI, the argument was that lower means better, and you're a real engineer if you make things yourself.
There's a ton of ego that comes with this rationale.
See, if you talk the talk, but can then back it up, then it's all good, I'm fine with that.
But none of those purists (people that write code) are able to back it up, ever.

Lets think about what it truly means to "create it yourself".

Take an average purist in development, and the process goes something like this:
+ Starts by writing his own C++ code, making him feel smarter than the unwashed masses.
- Writing it where, though? In his IDE program. Made by someone else.
- And where is the IDE running? On his operating system. Made by someone else.
- His code is colorful and readable because of a syntax highlighter. Made by someone else.
- The IDE tells him about mistakes before he even runs the code, thanks to an LSP, made by someone else.
- He clicks one of its autocomplete suggestions. Generated by software, made by someone else.
- The programming language itself? Designed and maintained by someone else.
- The standard library containing the basic functionality his program needs? Made by someone else.
- The framework that decides how half of his application is structured? Made by someone else.
- The documentation teaching him how to use all of this? Written by someone else.
- The forum answer he finds after copying an error message into a search engine? Written by someone else.
- The search engine that found it? Made by someone else.

Eventually, his creation is ready. The Holy CODE!

...But it needs to be compiled.

- Compiled by a compiler. Made by someone else.
- Linked by a linker. Made by someone else.
- Built through a build system. Made by someone else.
- Its dependencies are downloaded by a package manager. Made by someone else.
- Those dependencies come from repositories operated by someone else.
- Many of them contain algorithms invented by someone else.
- Those algorithms are built on mathematics discovered by someone else.

Now he needs to make sure his creation actually works.

- A formatter rewrites his code into the accepted style. Made by someone else.
- A debugger lets him pause the program and inspect its state. Made by someone else.
- A profiler tells him why it is slow. Made by someone else.
- A memory checker tells him why it crashes. Made by someone else.

The code works, so now it needs to be released.

- He stores it in a version-control system. Made by someone else.
- He pushes it to a hosting platform. Made by someone else.
- A continuous-integration system builds it. Made by someone else.
- A continuous-delivery system packages and deploys it. Made by someone else.
- Infrastructure is configured through automation tools made by someone else.

Now his code can finally run.

Except it isn't really his C++ code anymore.

The comments are gone.
The variable names are mostly gone.
The classes are gone.
The templates are gone.
The abstractions that made the program understandable to a human are gone.

What remains is a sequence of encoded processor instructions selected and rearranged by the compiler.

The processor fetches those instructions using circuitry designed by someone else.

At this point, even the machine code is an abstraction.

He wrote C++.
Someone else's compiler interpreted it.
Someone else's optimizer transformed it.
Someone else's instruction set represented it.
Someone else's operating system loaded it.
Someone else's processor reorganized and executed it.
Someone else's transistors responded to electrical fields according to laws of physics that none of us created.

And then he tells you he would never use TypeScript, because it's way too high-level for him.
He likes it close to the bone.

He pressed the keys himself, so apparently this is where creation happens.


---

You define the problem, direct the process, evaluate the output, reject the bad decisions, refine the good ones, integrate the result, fix it when it breaks, and take responsibility for your project, then you are developing.
